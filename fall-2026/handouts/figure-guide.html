Figure guide

This guide goes with meeting 11. Use it in session B to critique figures, and run it on every figure in your proposal, due December 11, and on every figure in your final presentation.

A chart made for a reader makes one point by showing numbers side by side, and the point stands out by contrast with something else. It is built in four steps, and each step asks four questions. A chart that fails a question in step 1 or step 4 leaves the reader without the message. A chart that fails a question in step 2 is read imprecisely, and one that fails in step 3 is read with a bias: the reader comes away believing something the numbers do not say. A diagram shows how the parts of an idea connect, and section 5 has its own questions.

Figures you make to explore your own data do not need any of this. Make many, quickly, and look at every one before you fit a model (meeting 5). This guide is for the few you show.

1. Decide what to contrast

Question What goes wrong What to do
1.1 What is the message, in one sentence? Everything is shown and nothing is contrasted Write the message before drawing, then draw only the relevant numbers
1.2 Contrasted with what? A change shown with no comparison group or no period before it Add the comparison group and the period before the change
1.3 Which quantity shows it? Nominal dollars, levels, two series or counts when the message needs real dollars, growth, the gap or a rate Deflate money by a price index; index to a base year; plot the gap; divide by the population
1.4 Which form shows it? A bar of means hides the distribution; a figure holds numbers a reader needs to look up Show the data points or the distribution (Weissgerber et al. 2015); put exact values in a table

To find a chart form, look up what you are comparing in the Financial Times Visual Vocabulary: deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, magnitude, part-to-whole, spatial or flow.

2. Encode it for the eye

A channel is the visual property that stands for a number: position, length, angle, area or color. Readers judge positions on a common scale more accurately than lengths, angles, areas or colors (Cleveland and McGill 1984; Heer and Bostock 2010).

Question What goes wrong What to do
2.1 Are the compared values on a common scale? A pie, a stacked bar or bubbles make close values hard to rank Put the values on one axis as dots or bars, sorted
2.2 Does the channel match the data? A rainbow color scale for ordered values; lines joining categories that have no order Use one hue, light to dark, for ordered values; use lines only for time or another ordered axis
2.3 Are the compared items next to each other? Many series tangled in one panel; compared groups on separate panels with different scales Draw small multiples on shared axes; put compared groups side by side on one scale
2.4 Do the points hide each other? Thousands of points overplotted into a blot Make the points transparent and add the mean of y within bins of x, a binned scatter (Cattaneo et al. 2024)

3. Keep it faithful

Question What goes wrong What to do
3.1 Does the scale match the quantity? A bar axis that skips zero; a log scale the reader takes for linear; an inverted axis; circles scaled by radius; 3D Start bars at zero; use a linear scale for a general reader; scale circles by area; draw in 2D
3.2 Do the window and the sample favor the message? Two y-axes whose ranges set where the lines cross; a time window that starts where the story looks best; a total that hides opposite subgroups; a sample that loses its failures over time Put compared series on one scale; show the full period or say why the window starts where it does; show the subgroups; say who left the sample
3.3 Is the uncertainty shown? Point estimates with no intervals; error bars that do not say what they are Draw 95% confidence intervals and say so in the caption; do not read overlapping intervals as no difference (Belia et al. 2005)
3.4 Does the title claim only what the figure shows? A causal title on a correlation Title the association; leave causal claims to a design (meetings 8 and 9)

4. Make it stand alone

Show the data, reduce the clutter, and integrate the text and the graph (Schwabish 2014).

Question What goes wrong What to do
4.1 Does the title state the finding? A label title (“Sales by region, 2020–2025”) Write the finding as the title; put the variables in a subtitle or the axis titles
4.2 Does every mark carry information? Heavy gridlines, borders, shading, markers, a legend of codes, variable names as axis titles Delete what carries no data; label lines where they end; write units in the axis titles; color one thing and leave the rest grey
4.3 Does the caption let the figure stand alone? “Figure 3: Effects” Give the source, the unit, the sample, the estimator and what the error bars are
4.4 Does it fit the page or the slide? A paper’s four-panel figure on a slide; red against green Redraw for the slide with one panel and large type; use a palette color-blind readers can tell apart, such as Okabe–Ito (Wong 2011)

Across a document, the same group keeps the same color in every figure.

5. Diagrams and builds

A causal graph, a research design and a data pipeline show how the parts of an idea connect. In a talk, a complex diagram builds one piece at a time, with one sentence for each piece. A paper’s reader sets the pace, so a paper prints the finished diagram (Larkin and Simon 1987; Mayer and Chandler 2001).

Question What goes wrong What to do
5.1 Which parts does the claim need? Every part of the project is drawn Cut any part that no sentence of the argument uses
5.2 Does each arrow style carry one meaning? Identical arrows mean “causes”, “comes before” and “is measured by” Give each meaning its own style and label it
5.3 Can you defend every arrow? A causal arrow nobody can justify; a missing arrow nobody questioned Say why each causal arrow is there and why each missing one is absent
5.4 Does it build in the order of the argument? The whole diagram arrives at once, and the room reads ahead Reveal one piece for each sentence; keep the axes and positions fixed from step to step

To build a figure in PowerPoint or Keynote, draw the finished version and give each piece an Appear animation. In Quarto, put the fragment class on each frame. In R, save one image per frame with the same axis limits.

Before you have results

A proposal has no estimates to plot. It can still draw three things, all from what you already have.

  • The conceptual model, as a causal graph.
  • The design, as a diagram: which units get the treatment and when.
  • The data: the sample as a grid of units by period, and the outcome before the treatment for the treated and the comparison group.

Do not draw results you do not have. Invented numbers in a proposal read as a result, however they are labelled.

Further reading

  • Schwabish (2014), “An economist’s guide to visualizing data”, Journal of Economic Perspectives. Short, free, and written for economists, with eight published figures redrawn.
  • Wilke (2019), Fundamentals of Data Visualization, free online. Organized by what you want to show.
  • Healy (2018), Data Visualization: A Practical Introduction, free online, with R code.
  • Zelazny (2001), Say It With Charts, 4th edition, McGraw-Hill. How to choose a chart from its message.
  • Case and Deaton (2015), “Rising morbidity and mortality in midlife among white non-Hispanic Americans in the 21st century”, PNAS. Figure 1 is a research figure worth studying.
  • Larkin and Simon (1987), “Why a diagram is (sometimes) worth ten thousand words”, Cognitive Science. What a diagram does for a reader.
  • Rosling (2006), “The best stats you’ve ever seen”, TED. A chart explained before it moves.
  • Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks and Hullman (2021), “The science of visual data communication: what works”, Psychological Science in the Public Interest. The experiments behind these rules.
