Team Y · Boycotts and store visits at Target, 2023 and 2025

Lena Park, Marco Silva, Nadia Haddad, Owen Brooks, Priya Natarajan

It asks a question a company would pay to answer. The solar farm example asks one a town would act on. Both follow the instructions section by section. 1. Research question

Did the 2023 and 2025 boycotts of Target lower visits to its stores most in the counties whose politics matched each boycott? This is an evaluation. A retailer’s marketing team, deciding where to spend on promotions to win shoppers back after a boycott, would act on the answer. The 2023 boycott came from conservative shoppers angry about Pride merchandise, and the 2025 boycott from shoppers opposed to Target ending its diversity goals. We expect visits to fall a few percent more in Republican-leaning counties in 2023, and in Democratic-leaning counties in 2025. We are wrong if visits fall by the same amount everywhere, or if the same counties fall most in both years.

2. Why it matters

Both boycotts fell in quarters when Target’s sales declined. Its comparable store sales fell 4.3 percent in the second quarter of 2023 and 5.7 percent in the first quarter of 2025 (Target Corporation 2023, 2025). In 2023 the company said it could not separate the effect of the reaction from other causes (Associated Press 2023b). If the losses concentrate in counties whose politics match the boycott, a marketing team can aim its promotions at those stores. If they do not, it spends nationally. A retailer weighing a public stance could also estimate in advance which stores it puts at risk, and for how long.

Two studies come closest to ours. Hou and Poliquin (2023) used phone location data to count visits to the stores of several large retailers whose chief executives backed expanding background checks for gun purchases. Visits fell about 3 percent over four weeks, about 5 percent in the most conservative counties and not at all in the most liberal ones, and the drop faded within 10 weeks. Liaukonytė, Tuchman and Zhu (2023) studied the boycott of Goya after its chief executive praised President Trump. A counter-movement of supporters outweighed it. Sales rose about 22 percent, and the rise was gone within three weeks. The increase was largest in heavily Republican counties, with no strong offsetting fall in heavily Democratic ones. Each study follows stances on one side of politics. We study one retailer boycotted from opposite sides two years apart, which lets us test whether the same stores move in opposite directions.

3. Approach

Each observation is one Target store in one week. The outcome is the logarithm of the store’s weekly visits, from Advan’s Weekly Patterns Plus data in Dewey. The sample covers all of Target’s nearly 2,000 stores in the United States, from January 2022 to December 2025, which spans both boycotts. These are the stores a marketing team would choose among.

The treatment is the boycott. Every store was exposed to it, so we split stores by the politics of their county, measured by the Republican share of the 2020 presidential vote. We compare stores in the third of counties with the highest share against stores in the third with the lowest. We date the 2023 boycott from the week of May 22, when Target pulled some Pride merchandise after confrontations in its stores. The collection had drawn misleading videos for weeks before that (Associated Press 2023a), so we leave out the weeks from its launch in early May to May 22. We date the 2025 boycott from the week of January 20, when Target announced it would end its diversity goals, and we mark the 40-day boycott that began on March 5 (Hopkins and Gray News staff 2025).

The comparison group for stores in the Republican third is the same chain’s stores in the Democratic third, and the reverse. Their visits stand in for the counterfactual, what each group’s visits would have done without the boycott. Both groups share Target’s prices, products and national advertising, so anything the chain did to every store drops out. The counties differ in income, density and much else, so we compare each store’s change in visits over time rather than its level. The design is difference-in-differences, run as an event study for each boycott. It compares the change in visits at stores in the Republican third with the change at stores in the Democratic third, week by week, from 20 weeks before each boycott to 20 weeks after. We make the comparison among counties of similar income, so that a shock hitting poorer counties does not show up as politics. This design measures how much more visits fell in one group of counties than in the other. It cannot measure the boycott’s total effect on Target, because every store was exposed. The identifying assumption is that, within each income group and without the boycotts, visits in the two groups of counties would have followed parallel paths.

The two boycotts also give us a check. If the boycotts drive the response, the gap between the two groups should change sign between 2023 and 2025. A shock that hits the same counties in both years, such as prices rising faster in poorer counties, would push the gap the same way both times. A shift in spending that follows county politics could flip it too, and section 5 explains how we separate that.

5. Anticipated challenges

The main threat is omitted variables: a shift in spending that follows county politics in the same weeks, with no boycott behind it. Our 2025 window opens in September 2024 and spans the presidential election and the inauguration. If shoppers in Democratic-leaning counties cut spending at every store after either event, Target’s gap would move the way the boycott predicts, and the sign check could not tell the two apart. That would make our 2025 estimate too large. Visits to other large chains in the same counties separate the two, because a boycott of Target lowers visits at Target and a general cut lowers them everywhere. We will use chains that sell different goods from Target and took no public stance on diversity goals. If their gap moves the same way as Target’s, we will report by February 15 that our 2025 estimate cannot separate the boycott from a partisan shift in spending.

In 2023, Target moved its Pride merchandise to the back of some Southern stores (Associated Press 2023a). If that softened the reaction where conservative shoppers are concentrated, it pushes our 2023 estimate toward zero. We cannot observe which stores made the change, so we will state it as a limitation in the outline by November 11.
