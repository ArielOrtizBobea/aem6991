Project description

Due on Canvas before meeting 9 starts. Meeting 9 is scheduled for Wednesday, October 21, at 2:00 pm. My travel may move it to October 20 or 22, and the deadline moves with it. The schedule shows the final date.

This is the first written piece of your team’s proposal. It replaces the one-page pitch announced in meetings 1 and 3.

The assignment in brief

What A two-page description of your project, and a five-minute talk about it
Who One submission per team
Weight 5 % of the course grade. Every member receives the same grade
Due On Canvas, before meeting 9 starts
Length Two pages for the six sections. A third page holds only the references and the AI disclosure
File One PDF, named Team-A_project-description.pdf with your team’s letter
Talk Five minutes at meeting 9, followed by questions from the room

What the document must contain

The first line of page 1 gives your team letter and a working title. The second line lists every member’s full name. A name on that line means the member has read the document and agrees with it.

Then use the six headings below, numbered and worded as they are here, in this order. Each one gives a rough length and the session-B sheet to start from.

1. Research question

About five lines. Start from your meeting 3 slate.

  • State the question in one sentence ending with a question mark. It is the first sentence of the section.
  • Name its shape from meeting 3: evaluation, preference and pricing, prediction and risk, diagnosis, feasibility, measurement, or valuation.
  • Name who would act on the answer or pay for it, by role. “The pricing manager of a regional grocery chain” is specific enough. “Policymakers” is too vague.
  • State the answer you expect, and the result that would show you were wrong.

2. Why it matters

About a quarter of a page. Start from the value line on your meeting 3 slate.

  • Name the decision that would change with the answer, and who makes it.
  • Summarize the two or three studies closest to yours, one sentence each, and say what your project adds. Meeting 5 showed how to find them.
  • Cite each study by author and year, and list it on page 3.

3. Approach

About a third of a page, plus your causal graph if you include one. Start from your meeting 2 graph and your meeting 8 design sheet.

Every project states these three things.

  • Name the unit of observation, such as a store-week or a household.
  • Name the outcome, the variable that measures it, and its source.
  • Describe the sample: which units, where, and which years.

If your question asks what a change caused, state these four as well.

  • Name the treatment, the change whose effect you want to estimate.
  • Describe the counterfactual, the comparison group or period that stands in for what would have happened without the treatment.
  • Name the research design, such as an experiment, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, or panel data with fixed effects.
  • State the identifying assumption in one sentence. It is the assumption that makes your comparison a valid stand-in for the counterfactual.

If your question asks for a prediction or a measurement, state this instead.

  • Say what the prediction or measurement has to get right to be useful, and how you will check it, for example by out-of-sample prediction error.

4. Data and access

About half a page. Start from your meeting 3 resource map. I read this section first, because most capstones that fail had a good question whose data never arrived.

Give one row per dataset, in a table with these six columns.

Dataset Holder Unit and period Variables you need Access time Status
Example: Weekly Patterns Plus Dewey, through Cornell’s subscription Store-week, 2019–2024 Weekly visits, store category, location Days File opened Oct 12
  • Access time is your dataset’s row on the access ladder: now, days, weeks, months, or longer.
  • Status is one of four entries, each with its date: file opened, access requested, holder contacted, or not started.

Below the table, add three things.

  • Give the date by which you expect to hold every dataset.
  • Give your plan B, the question your project becomes if the data has not arrived by that date.
  • Say whether the project needs IRB review or a data-use agreement, using your meeting 4 IRB and DUA checklist. If it does, give the date the slowest approval has to start.

5. Anticipated challenges

About a quarter of a page. Start from your meeting 7 threat sheet.

Name two or three challenges. For each one, say what you will do if it is not resolved, and by what date. One of them must be the main threat to your estimate.

  • For a causal question, name the source of bias, the direction in which it pushes the estimate, and whether you can measure the variable that would remove it.
  • For a prediction or a measurement, name the main way it could be wrong.

The other challenges can be the row of your resource map most likely to stop the project, or an approval you are still waiting for.

6. Team plan

About half a page, in two parts. Start from your meeting 2 ground rules.

Roles. Give one row per member, in a table with these five columns. Each Delivers cell names a specific output. Each By cell gives a date before December 11. Name a coordinator, who runs the weekly check-in and uploads the team’s work. Name a backup for every main task. For example:

Member Role Delivers By Backup
Ana Coordinator Runs the check-in. Uploads each deliverable Every Wednesday Ben
Ben Data Dewey account approved. First extract opened and described Oct 30 Carla
Carla Design Causal graph and identifying assumption, written for the outline Nov 4 Dev
Dev Literature Three anchor papers, each opened, and what ours adds Nov 4 Eva
Eva Writing Outline merged and edited into one voice Nov 10 Ana

Missed work. Write the steps your team follows when a member does not deliver. A miss is a task not done by its date without warning. A member who warns the team before the date has not missed it, and the team sets a new date at the next check-in. Give at least three numbered steps, ending with the team bringing the problem to me. For example:

  1. First miss. It is raised at the next check-in and written down, with a new date.
  2. Second miss. The coordinator talks to the member that week. The task goes to the backup or is split, and the check-in records it.
  3. No change, or a week with no reply. The team brings it to me, with the check-ins as the record.

Page 3: references and AI disclosure

Page 3 holds only these two items.

References. List every work you cite, in any consistent citation style, each with a link or DOI. Cite only works you have opened. A citation to a work that does not exist is treated as fabrication under the AI policy.

AI disclosure. Write the three lines from meeting 5: the tools you used, what you used them for, and what you checked. If you used none, write “Tools: none.” For example:

Tools. Claude (claude.ai).
Used for. Finding candidate anchor papers. Tightening the wording of section 2.
Checked. We opened every reference and wrote each summary ourselves.

Format and submission

  • Use letter paper, one-inch margins, and 11-point type or larger. Single spacing is fine. Tables and figure labels may use 9-point type.
  • Fit sections 1 to 6, including tables and figures, on two pages. I do not read anything past page 2 except the references and the AI disclosure on page 3. Do not add appendices.
  • Name the file Team-A_project-description.pdf, with your team’s letter.
  • One member uploads it to the Canvas group assignment for the team. I do not accept submissions by email.
  • You may resubmit before the deadline. I grade the last version.
  • The syllabus late policy applies. A late submission loses 10 % up to 8 hours late and 50 % from 8 to 72 hours. After 72 hours it is not accepted.

The talk at meeting 9

  • Every team presents. The order is drawn at random, so be ready from the start of session B.
  • You have five minutes, timed, and I stop you at five. Questions from the room follow.
  • Cover the question, who acts on the answer, your approach, and your data with its status. For a causal question, the approach includes the design and its identifying assumption.
  • Your team decides who speaks.
  • Slides are optional, three at most. Upload them to the same Canvas assignment by the same deadline, as Team-A_slides.pdf. I show them from the room computer.

How it is graded

The document and the talk are scored together out of 100. That score is worth 5 % of the course grade, and every member of the team receives it.

Part Points
1. Research question 15
2. Why it matters 10
3. Approach 15
4. Data and access 20
5. Anticipated challenges 10
6. Team plan 10
The talk 20
Total 100

Each part is scored on the syllabus scale. Excellent earns full points. Adequate earns three quarters, weak earns half, and a missing part earns zero. The talk is scored on whether it covers the four items within five minutes, and on how the team answers questions.

Questions students ask

Can we change our question after this? Yes. Record the change and the reason in your team’s decision log.

We do not have the data yet. Is that a problem? No. Show what you have checked and requested, with dates, and give your plan B. A dataset you have named but not checked scores weak.

Our question is a prediction or a measurement. Do we need a research design? No. Complete the last part of section 3 instead.

We expect to use difference-in-differences, which is taught at meeting 9. What do we write? Name the design and describe the comparison in plain words. Remix chapters 8 and 9 cover it, and you refine it in the outline.

Can the team lower a member’s grade for not contributing? No. Every member receives the same grade on team work. Follow your missed-work steps, which end with the team bringing the problem to me.

Why do we have to write roles and a missed-work procedure? Writing them down early is standard practice, and there is evidence for it. Mathieu and Rapp followed 32 teams of MBA students through a business strategy simulation. The teams that sustained the best performance had written both a good team charter and a good plan for the work. Oakley and colleagues give a template for both.
