3 · Finding the question

Wednesday, September 9, 2026

This page is a stub. Materials appear here before the meeting.

What you should be able to do after this meeting. Turn an interest, or a dataset you did not choose, into a question somebody could answer, and say which archetype it is. Break a question that is not one by naming the filter it fails.

Session A · 2:05–3:15

Three blocks. First, where ideas come from. Five routes: a gap in what is known, extending or contesting a published result, an anomaly, a decision somebody actually has to make, and a dataset nobody has looked at properly. Route five is the most dangerous, and we come back to why. Then the framing that matters. Most of your education has rewarded execution. This course rewards the part before it. A bad question executed beautifully is still a bad project. It is also the most enjoyable thing you will do all semester, so do not rush it. Second, whether there is a method for having ideas. Partly. Two of the popular answers have real evidence behind them, and most do not. Start from your means, not your goal. That is effectuation, from Sarasvathy’s think-aloud studies of expert entrepreneurs. Experts do not pick a goal and hunt for the means. They start from who they are, what they know and whom they know, and work forward. The second is simpler. Generate, then judge, and never both at once. Teams that evaluate while brainstorming produce fewer ideas, and safer ones. For research questions specifically, Sandberg and Alvesson separate gap-spotting from problematization. For one semester, gap-spotting is usually right. Knowing which one you are doing is what stops you overclaiming. A caution that applies here as everywhere in this course: being taught in every business school is not evidence that something works. Design thinking as a branded five-step method is widely taught and lightly tested. So is lean startup. Third, the shapes a project can take. Five archetypes: evaluation, preference and pricing, prediction and risk, diagnosis and benchmarking, feasibility and landscape. For each, what it can claim, what design it needs, and how it fails. Evaluation asks whether it worked, and dies without a credible comparison group. Did the loyalty relaunch change basket size? Preference and pricing asks what people would choose and pay, and dies when stated preference gets reported as revealed preference. Prediction and risk asks what happens next, and dies when the team quietly starts making causal recommendations off a predictive model. Diagnosis asks where the gap is, and dies as description dressed up as a cause. Feasibility asks whether the opportunity is real, and dies as a deck of secondary research. A project that cannot pick one of the five is not yet a project. Then the last two cohorts, on one slide. Four of the five 2026 projects were the same project. Take a public signal anybody can download, regress a financial outcome on it, report a coefficient. Free data, no design, no decision attached. I let the room find that rather than saying it, because it lands harder from a student than from me. Then six anti-patterns. The topic with no question. The dataset in search of a question. The consulting deck. The unobtainable-data project. The dissertation. The already-answered question. And finally the filter, which is four questions. Could a team of three to five execute this in one spring semester? Can you name the specific dataset, who holds it, and how you get it, checked rather than assumed? Could the answer come out either way? Would anybody act differently depending on it? A no on any one is fatal, and it is cheap to discover today.

Session B · 3:30–4:30

Every session B opens with a ten-minute team check-in: what you did since last week, what is stuck, and who is doing what next. Written down, and handed in with that session’s work.

Ten-minute team check-in first. Then the question relay, against the clock. Nine dataset cards go out and your team holds one it did not choose. Real estate transactions. Climate risk by ZIP. Weekly foot traffic. EV chargers. Job postings with salaries. WARN layoff notices. Convenience store scanner data. Card spend by brand. CDC PLACES. Write the three best questions that data could answer, pick your best, put it at the top of the sheet, and label its archetype. Then the datasets pass one way and the question sheets pass the other, so no team ever gets its own question back. You break the question you inherit by naming which of the four filters kills it, or you write SURVIVES, honestly. Then you write a new question on a dataset you have not seen. In the last round you may combine any two datasets your team has held. I say the join keys out loud before that round starts. Parcels, stores and chargers carry lat/long. Postings and layoffs carry company. Scanner and card data carry brand. The good questions live in the joins. Scoring: one point every time a question of yours survives a break, one point for every break you land, two points for the best combination question. Saying a question is weak is not a break. You have to name the filter. Everybody works the same nine cards, so the kills are comparable, and I read out the two best combination questions and one good kill at the end. The kill goes last, because the killing is the lesson. Then your own project, which is the half you hand in. Three candidate questions for what your team actually wants to work on. Each labelled with an archetype. Each run through the four filters in writing, not in your heads. One of the three has to be crossed out with the filter that killed it named, because a team that kills nothing has not used the filter. That page is your question slate. It is submitted before you leave, it is what your pitch gets built from, and a question you kill in September is a month you do not lose in March.
