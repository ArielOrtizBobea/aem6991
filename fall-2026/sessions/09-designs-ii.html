Handouts

Project description brief

Where to read more

Remix ch. 8 on panel data and fixed effects, ch. 9 on difference-in-differences, ch. 10 if your rollout is staggered, and ch. 11 on synthetic control. The Effect ch. 16 to 18 cover fixed effects, event studies and difference-in-differences. Mostly Harmless ch. 5.

Session A · 2:00–3:15

Designs that use change over time, each drawn before it is described. Fixed effects first, opened by women on corporate boards, where the association with firm value changed sign once each firm was compared with itself: what the fixed effects remove, the confounders that change over time and survive them, measurement error that they amplify, and a common shortcut that is not a fixed effect. Then difference-in-differences and event studies as one design built in four steps. Two groups and two periods, opened by John Snow’s comparison of two London water companies before and after one of them moved its intake in 1852: cholera deaths fell by 57 per 10,000 residents in the districts it partly supplied, relative to districts it did not reach. A simulated coffee chain that launches a mobile-ordering app shows what each subtraction removes and what parallel trends assumes, and two studies show it failing: trainees whose earnings dipped just before job training, and Philadelphia’s beverage tax pushing sales into the stores outside the city. More periods turn the comparison into an event study, whose pre-trend tests can pass when parallel trends fails. Adoption on different dates changes which units serve as controls, and two-way fixed effects can then report the wrong sign. Treatment that comes in doses needs a stronger assumption to compare one dose with another. Then few treated units: standard errors with one treated state, and synthetic control for California’s 1988 tobacco program. Last, the finance event study, opened by stock prices on the day of earnings announcements: the market model, abnormal returns, and other news in the window. The session closes with all six designs of meetings 8 and 9 in one table.

Session B · 3:30–4:30

Every session B opens with a ten-minute team check-in: what you did since last week, what is stuck, and who is doing what next. Written down, and handed in with that session’s work.

Seven published studies that cover the designs of meetings 8 and 9, each posed as the question and the setting its authors faced: working from home at a Chinese travel agency, family heirs as chief executives, close shareholder votes on governance, calorie labels at Starbucks, bank deregulation and inequality, the cost of terrorism in the Basque Country, and the sudden deaths of company directors. For each, the room proposes a design and names the assumption it needs before the slide shows what the authors did. Then teams are drawn at random to put up their own project’s design, draw the graph, state the assumption carrying the estimate, and defend it while the room tries to break it. Every team presents at some point, without notice. The design that survives goes in your identification section, and the attack that landed goes in your limitations paragraph.

Your team’s output is submitted before you leave.

Practice questions

These 10 questions practice the ideas of this lecture. Click an option to check it. A wrong pick lets you try again, and the right one links to the slide that covers it. Four of them ask for a short written answer, as the quiz will. Write yours down before you open the model answer. The quiz on this lecture asks different questions on the same ideas.

1. On 1 March an airline starts a seat-upgrade offer on its Gulf Coast routes and on no other routes. The airline estimates the effect of the offer by difference-in-differences: the change in revenue per flight on the Gulf Coast routes from before 1 March to after, minus the change on the airline's other routes over the same months. In March a rival airline stops flying the Gulf Coast routes. The rival never flew the airline's other routes. In which direction does the rival's exit push the estimate, relative to the true effect of the offer?

2. A clothing chain sends a turnaround team to a store after the store has an unusually poor year. To estimate the effect of a turnaround team on sales, the chain regresses each store's change in sales from one year to the next on an indicator for getting a turnaround team between the two years, with no controls. The coefficient on the indicator is large and positive. Which change to the design best identifies the true effect of a turnaround team?

3. On 1 May a state starts paying hiring subsidies to firms in 10 of its counties. Firms can respond by moving jobs into the 10 subsidized counties from the counties that adjoin them. To estimate the effect of the subsidies on jobs, an analyst uses difference-in-differences: the change in monthly jobs in the 10 subsidized counties from before 1 May to after, minus the change in a group of control counties over the same months. Which counties should the analyst use as controls?

4. A pet-food brand chooses one state for a promotion, and stores in other states are the control group. The brand runs an event study of each store's monthly sales, with store and month fixed effects. The leads mark each month before the promotion in the promotion state, and the lags mark each month after the promotion. The estimated effect is positive, and its 95% confidence interval excludes zero if parallel trends holds. The lead coefficients change by up to $20 from one month to the next. The confidence interval includes zero once the trends of the two groups are allowed to diverge by $10 a month. How firm is the estimated effect?

5. Property trusts whose shares trade on a stock exchange announce 60 acquisitions. To estimate how an acquisition announcement changes a trust's market value, an analyst runs a finance event study. The analyst fits a market model for each trust, computes the cumulative abnormal return from the day before each announcement to the day after, and averages the 60 cumulative abnormal returns. In 15 of the 60 event windows, the trust also announces a higher dividend. What should the analyst do with those 15 events?

6. A cinema chain whose shares trade on a stock exchange has 100 theaters. In June the chain ends free drink refills at 40 of the theaters and keeps free refills at the other 60. To estimate the effect of ending free refills on admissions, an analyst regresses each theater's monthly admissions on leads and lags, with theater and month fixed effects. The leads mark each month before June at the 40 theaters, and the lags mark each month from June on. In this design, what supplies the counterfactual for the 40 theaters?

7. A health network has 80 clinics. Over 36 months, 50 of the clinics started nurse triage, each in a month the clinic chose, and they were mostly clinics with sicker patients. The other 30 clinics never started nurse triage. The network regresses each clinic's average waiting time in a month on an indicator for the clinic having nurse triage in that month, with clinic fixed effects and month fixed effects. What assumption must hold for the coefficient on the indicator to be the true effect of nurse triage on waiting times?

Write your answer in one or two sentences, then compare.

Show a model answer

Strict exogeneity: nothing that changes within a clinic over time drives both nurse triage and waiting times, and past waiting times do not drive the month a clinic starts. Stated as parallel trends, the same assumption reads: without nurse triage, waiting times at the clinics that started would have changed as they did at the clinics that had not started. Either statement is a right answer. The clinic fixed effects remove a clinic's fixed patient mix, so sicker patients alone do not break the assumption. Because the clinics started in different months, the coefficient also needs an effect that does not change with time since a clinic started. If the effect builds over time, an estimator made for staggered timing is needed.

Slide: Fixed effects remove unchanging confounders.

8. A county has 25 towns. In April 2024 the county extended bus service to its 8 largest towns and to none of the other 17 towns. An analyst has monthly store visits for each town, for the 36 months before April 2024 and the 12 months after. The analyst uses difference-in-differences: the change in store visits in the 8 bus towns from before April 2024 to after, minus the change in the other 17 towns over the same months. What assumption must hold for this difference-in-differences to give the true effect of the bus service on store visits?

Write your answer in one or two sentences, then compare.

Show a model answer

Parallel trends: without the bus service, store visits in the 8 bus towns would have changed by the same amount as store visits in the other 17 towns. The higher level of visits in the bus towns does not matter, since the design compares changes. The bus service must also not pull shoppers away from the other 17 towns. A plot of both groups over the 36 months before April 2024 is the check.

Slide: Two subtractions remove two kinds of confounder.

9. A water utility has 85 districts. In June 2024 the 35 districts that share one billing system began texting payment reminders to customers. The other 50 districts never sent reminders. The utility has the number of late payments in each district in each month from 2023 to 2025. The utility wants to know how the effect of the reminders on late payments changes over the months after June 2024. Which research design fits this situation?

Write your answer in one or two sentences, then compare.

Show a model answer

A panel event study: district and month fixed effects, with an indicator for the reminder districts in each month before and after June 2024, measured against a reference month before June 2024. The design compares the 35 reminder districts with the other 50 districts, month by month. Parallel trends must hold in every month, with no anticipation: without the reminders, late payments in the two groups of districts would have moved together. Flat leads are the check. The lags show whether the effect arrives at once or builds. A difference-in-differences uses the same comparison and gives one average effect over the months after June 2024, so it does not show how the effect changes.

Slide: An event study estimates the effect month by month.

10. A cable firm serves 30 counties. In 2023 a rival entered one of the 30 counties and none of the other 29. The cable firm has its monthly subscribers in each county since 2013. From 2013 to 2022, the firm's subscribers in the county the rival entered grew unevenly, and slower than in the average county. Over those years, subscribers in that county grew slower than in the county nearest in size and faster than in the county nearest in income. The firm wants the effect of the rival's entry on its subscribers in that county. Which research design fits this situation?

Write your answer in one or two sentences, then compare.

Show a model answer

Synthetic control. The design compares the county the rival entered with a weighted mix of the other 29 counties. The weights are non-negative, sum to one, and are chosen so the mix tracks the county's monthly subscribers from 2013 to 2022. The effect is the gap from 2023 on. The mix must be one that would have kept tracking the county without the rival. The rival's entry must not affect the other 29 counties, and the firm must not have reacted before 2023. The checks are the fit before 2023 and a placebo in space.

Slide: California's tobacco program had no matching state.
