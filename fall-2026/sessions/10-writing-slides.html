1. Your readers

Thirty seconds. Who reads which part, and at what level of detail.

Most panelists read only part of a grant application

  • The National Institutes of Health (NIH) funds US medical research through review panels.
  • About 20 panelists score each application.
  • At least three assigned reviewers read it closely.
  • The rest most likely skim the abstract, the one-page Specific Aims and the significance section.
  • One NIH institute tells applicants to write those parts for a reader of Scientific American, a popular science magazine.

National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID), Know Your Audience, NIH grant guidance.

Two minutes.

NIH is the largest public funder of medical research in the world, and its review panels are where most US research proposals are judged. The institute’s advice describes the panel as it is: around 20 people score each application, three of them were assigned to read it, and everyone else forms a view from the parts they had time for.

The people who vote are mostly not the people who read closely. A proposal has to work for both. The parts the skimmers read are written for an intelligent reader outside the field, which is why NIAID names a popular science magazine as the level.

Readers range from skimmers to deep readers

In research In an organization
Skimmers read the title and the summary, then stop Someone searching for papers; a student The executive who needs the big picture
Engaged readers read the introduction and the main results Someone writing a literature review; a researcher deciding whether the paper matters to them The manager who acts on the recommendation

  • Some details are never read, and they are written for the few who need them.

Two minutes.

Every document has all three kinds of reader, in different proportions. In research, most people who meet a paper see its title in a search or a reference list. Fewer read the introduction to decide whether it matters to them. A handful read every line: the referee, a critic, the researcher who builds on it.

In an organization the summary is for the executive, who needs the big picture and will not read further. The detail is for the people who check the work. Some of it is never read, and that is by design: it is there so the few who need it can find it.

Each part of a paper has its own reader

  • The title reaches skimmers. It has no jargon and no acronyms.
  • The abstract reaches skimmers and engaged readers. Each technical term is spelled out.
  • The introduction reaches engaged readers. It uses the field’s terms and defines each one.
  • The methods and appendix reach deep readers. They carry the full technical detail.
  • The technical level rises as the reader goes deeper.

One and a half minutes.

The common advice to “write for a general audience” is half right. The title and the abstract are written for the broadest reader. The deeper sections match the deeper reader, who would be slowed down by explanations of what they already know.

Two pieces of evidence behind writing the top layers plainly. George Heilmeier, who ran the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) in the 1970s, asked of every proposal: “What are you trying to do? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon.” And in an economics experiment, Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989, https://doi.org/10.1086/261651) found that people who know something overestimate how much others know, which they called the curse of knowledge. You are the informed party in your own project.

Same lead author, same firm, two journals. The economics paper is framed around a question economists care about, incentives: does an employee out of sight shirk? Choose the journal, or the reader, first. The framing of the contribution follows from it: which question the work answers, and which literature it speaks to. Your proposal’s reader is fixed, so the framing should be too.

2. Nested versions

Thirty seconds. Several kinds of reader means several versions of the same paper, nested one inside the next.

Internists reported reading only the abstract of most articles

  • In 1998, 264 of 399 US internists (doctors of internal medicine) returned a mailed survey on their journal reading.
  • They reported reading only the abstract for 63% of the articles they read.
  • For those articles, the abstract was the paper.

Saint, Christakis, Saha, Elmore, Welsh, Baker and Koepsell (2000), Journal reading habits of internists, Journal of General Internal Medicine 15(12), 881–884.

One minute.

These are practising doctors, who read journals to decide how to treat patients. For nearly two articles in three, what they took away came from the abstract alone. Self-reports, and from 1998, but the direction is not in doubt: most readers of most papers stop early.

What is each of these?

Three minutes. The slide opens on the three title dolls. Each click adds a column: the abstracts, then the introductions, then the full papers.

Ask what each title doll is before the first click. The cat is a cat from the title on: the pointed ears survive at every size, and each larger doll only adds detail. The dog is the same, with its floppy ears.

The third is the one to dwell on. Round ears and a brown body: a bear, or a monkey? Ask again when the abstracts appear, since the brown doll still has no tail. The introductions answer it with the tail.

The dolls are Russian nesting dolls, matryoshkas. Each smaller doll is the paper at a shorter length. A reader who stops at the title should still name the right animal.

A title should fit only your paper

  • Without the tail, the two small dolls fit a bear as well as a monkey.
  • A topic title fits many papers:

Climate change and global agricultural productivity

  • The published title fits one: “Anthropogenic climate change has slowed global agricultural productivity growth”
  • Each version keeps the feature that identifies the paper.

Doumont (2002), The three laws of professional communication, IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication 45(4), 291–296.

Two minutes.

The tail is the detail that separates the monkey from a bear. Leave it out of the title and the abstract and a skimmer leaves with the wrong animal. In a paper the tail is usually the finding, the setting or the design: whatever separates your paper from its neighbours.

Jean-luc Doumont, who trains scientists and engineers to communicate, calls this effective redundancy: each version retells the whole message, at another level of detail, in other words. Repeat only what the evidence supports, since repeated statements are rated more true whether or not they are (Dechêne, Stahl, Hansen and Wänke 2010, https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309352251).

The shortest version reaches the most readers

  • Economics journals cap abstracts at 100 words (American Economic Review, Journal of Finance) or 150 (Econometrica).
  • The US Government Accountability Office (GAO) publishes each report as Fast Facts, a two-page Highlights and the full report.

AER submission guidelines; Journal of Finance submission guidelines; Econometrica information for authors; GAO, for example GAO-24-107146.

One and a half minutes.

The pyramid of readers is upside down: many see the title, fewer read the abstract, fewer the introduction, and a few read the whole paper. The lengths on the right are the 2021 paper’s own; the band widths are drawn for illustration. The version with the most readers has the fewest words, and those words carry the message.

3. The structure readers expect

Thirty seconds. Each genre has a standard order. Readers know it and look for each part where it usually sits.

Researchers put shuffled paragraphs back in the right section

  • Dillon took two articles from a journal in the participants’ own field and removed every second paragraph.
  • Twelve human factors researchers sorted the rest, shuffled, into introduction, method, results and discussion.
  • They put 82.6% in the right section.
  • A common error confused the introduction with the discussion.

Dillon (1991), Readers’ models of text structures: the case of academic articles, International Journal of Man-Machine Studies 35(6), 913–925.

One minute.

Experienced readers carry the template in their heads. They knew where a paragraph belonged from its content alone, without headings or neighbours. A reader who knows where to look skims fast, and a document that puts things somewhere else costs that reader time. The confusion between introduction and discussion makes sense: both talk about the literature and what the result means.

How an empirical economics paper is organized

  1. TitleStates the finding
  2. AbstractThe question, the data and design, the finding with its size
  3. IntroductionThe funnel to the question, the finding and its size, the closest papers
  4. Setting and dataWhere and when, and what one row describes
  5. Empirical strategyThe design and its identifying assumption: what must hold for the estimate to measure the effect
  6. ResultsEach table introduced by its point
  7. Threats to validityWhat could break the design, and the checks
  8. ConclusionThe message again, shorter
  • The red boxes are the versions most readers stop at.

Dudenhefer (2014), A Guide to Writing in Economics, Duke University. Head, The Introduction Formula.

One and a half minutes.

The standard order for an applied paper. The first three boxes carry the whole message; the rest carry the evidence. Head’s formula puts the question by paragraph two or three of the introduction, then the antecedents, then the contribution.

Cochrane’s writing tips drop the funnel and open with the contribution in the first paragraph. Both put the answer in the introduction, well before the conclusion.

The writing guide has this schematic and four others: the proposal, the memo, the executive summary and the research talk.

How an applied theory paper is organized

  1. TitleStates the finding
  2. AbstractThe question, the model’s prediction, the finding
  3. IntroductionThe funnel to the question, the finding, the closest papers
  4. Theoretical frameworkA model that yields the testable prediction
  5. Empirical frameworkThe regression for each part of the prediction
  6. DataAnd descriptive statistics
  7. ResultsEach estimate read back through the model
  8. ConclusionThe message again, shorter
  • The model comes before the evidence and says what to estimate.
  • Bellemare’s rule: the model’s prediction maps one to one onto the empirical framework.
  • Ortiz-Bobea (2020) studied how climate affects US farmland. The usual estimate regresses farmland prices on climate across counties.
  • The model values farmland as farming income plus the option to convert it to other uses.
  • It predicts that farmland prices and cash rents give the same climate effect only where conversion is unlikely.
  • The empirical sections estimate the effect from prices, then from rents, and compare them.

Bellemare (2018), Between the Introduction and the Conclusion: The “Middle Bits” Formula for Applied Papers, blog post; expanded in Doing Economics (MIT Press, 2022). Ortiz-Bobea (2020), The Role of Nonfarm Influences in Ricardian Estimates of Climate Change Impacts on US Agriculture, American Journal of Agricultural Economics 102(3), 934–959.

Two minutes.

Some papers open with a model. The model is short, and its job is to say which quantities the data must deliver. Bellemare, an agricultural economist who writes about how applied papers are built, puts the theoretical framework right after the introduction and asks that its main prediction map one to one onto the empirical framework. If the model predicts something the regressions never test, cut it.

My 2020 paper is an example. The Ricardian approach regresses farmland prices on climate across counties and reads the climate coefficients as the value of climate to farming. The conceptual framework prices farmland as the value of farming it plus a premium for converting it to other uses, such as housing, and lists the predictions that follow. Estimates from farmland prices and from cash rents should agree where conversion is unlikely, and diverge where it looms. The price-to-rent ratio flags the counties where nonfarm influences are large. The empirical sections test exactly these: estimates from prices, then from cash rents, then a discussion that reconciles the two.

A second example is Gammans, Mérel and Ortiz-Bobea (2025, American Journal of Agricultural Economics, https://doi.org/10.1111/ajae.12491), on planting soybeans right after the winter wheat harvest. Its conceptual framework splits the effect of climate on crop revenue into an adaptation term and two yield terms, and each later section estimates one piece.

Your proposal has a heading for this: the conceptual model. It plays the same role, at a smaller scale: it says what you will estimate and why.

Two minutes.

The funnel is the opening that moves from the field to your question. Each band is narrower than the one above it, and the last one is the question. Here the first paragraph does the first three bands in five sentences; paragraph 2 says why earlier work on crop yields and GDP does not answer it; paragraph 3 opens with the question.

The test in the dashed box: a reader who stops after what is missing should already be able to say your question. Try it on the room before showing the last band. If they can, the funnel works and the question lands as confirmation.

A funnel is short. A reader should reach the question within a few sentences. For your proposal, the top of the funnel is the decision your reader faces, as meeting 1 said, and a gap in the literature comes in only where it matters to that decision.

  2. Bottom lineThe conclusion and the decision asked for

  4. EvidenceMost important first

Page one of the “Target boycott” proposal

  • In 2023 conservative shoppers boycotted Target over its Pride merchandise. In 2025 shoppers boycotted it after it ended its diversity goals.
What we will learn How much more store visits fell in counties whose politics matched each boycott
Why it is worth knowing A retailer’s marketing team decides where to spend to win shoppers back
How we will know Compare stores in the most and least Republican thirds of counties, week by week, 20 weeks either side of each boycott
  • This is difference-in-differences: the change in one group against the change in another. It assumes both groups would have followed parallel paths without the boycotts.
  • The gap should flip sign between 2023 and 2025.

Written for this slide from the “Target boycott” project description.

Two minutes.

The three answers fit in three lines, and a reviewer who reads only this page knows what the team will learn, who cares, and why the answer can be trusted.

Two details from the project description that page one leaves for later. The comparison is made within groups of counties of similar income, so a shock to poorer counties does not show up as politics. And every store was exposed to both boycotts, so the design measures a relative gap between groups of counties, which is smaller than the boycott’s total effect on Target. The flip in sign is the check: a shock that hits the same counties both times would push the gap the same way twice.

4. One idea per paragraph

Thirty seconds. The unit below the section is the paragraph, and each one carries one point.

Each paragraph makes one point

  • A topic sentence states the paragraph’s point. The other sentences support it.
  • Body paragraphs open with their point. The opening funnel of an introduction ends with it.
  • A reverse outline checks a draft: write one sentence summing up each paragraph.
  • A paragraph that needs two sentences holds two ideas. Split it.
  • A summary that matches a later sentence means the point is buried. Move that sentence up.

One and a half minutes.

Meeting 5 wrote the outline before the prose: one topic sentence per paragraph, in order. The reverse outline runs the same tool on a draft. Read each paragraph and write the one sentence it argues. Then read the summaries in order. If they make the argument, the draft works. If a summary is hard to write, the paragraph has no point yet.

A skimmer who reads only first sentences should get the argument, as you did with the 2021 introduction a few slides back.

    The whole introduction of the paper the room just summarized, as it appears in the article. First sentences are underlined. Hovering a sentence shows what it does; hovering a row on the right highlights that paragraph, scrolls to it, and marks the sentence that carries its point. Hovering a role in the legend highlights every sentence with that role.

    5. Keeping the reader on your path

    Thirty seconds. You choose what the reader reads and in what order. You cannot choose what they think along the way.

    You choose the reader’s path

    • You decide what the reader reads and in what order. The reader’s thoughts along the way are theirs.
    • In laboratory studies, adults read texts and were asked at random moments where their mind was.
    • Minds wandered 20 to 30% of the time. More wandering went with lower comprehension.

    D’Mello and Mills (2021), Mind wandering during reading: An interdisciplinary and integrative review, Language and Linguistics Compass 15(4), e12412. Bonifacci, Viroli, Vassura, Colombini and Desideri (2023), The relationship between mind wandering and reading comprehension: A meta-analysis, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 30(1), 40–59 (CC BY 4.0).

    Two minutes.

    Writing is a walk with your reader. You lay the path: what comes first, what comes next, where it ends. You cannot control where the reader looks along the way. A reader may wander off on a side trail, wondering about a criticism, a variation or a limitation, and some never come back.

    The evidence on drifting is strong. D’Mello and Mills review the laboratory work, where readers are probed at random moments; minds wandered 20 to 30% of the time. Bonifacci and coauthors pool 25 studies and find a correlation of about −0.21 between mind wandering and comprehension. Harder texts draw more of it.

    Umberto Eco described reading a story as a walk in the woods: the reader chooses at each fork, and the text makes some routes more reasonable than others (Six Walks in the Fictional Woods, 1994).

    Where would a skeptical reader stop following?

    We ask whether a new solar farm lowers the sale prices of homes within one kilometer2 of it in upstate New York3. Our sample covers the 431 farms that began operating between 2016 and 2023. Developers often build on cheap farmland at the edge of a village.1 We compare prices near each farm with prices 2 to 5 kilometers away, before and after it opens.

    1. A criticism. Were prices at the village edge already growing more slowly?

    2. A variation. Does a farm screened by trees lower prices as much?

    3. A limitation. Would this hold outside upstate New York?

    Written for this slide from the solar project description.

    Two and a half minutes. The bubbles appear one at a time.

    Before showing them, ask the room where they would stop and wonder. Then the three: a criticism of the design, a variation the reader wants to know about, and a limit on where the result applies.

    A skeptical reader does not wait for the limitations section. People resisting a persuasive message mostly argue back in their heads, and in studies of how people resist persuasion, counterarguing was both the most common strategy and the one that worked best (Jacks and Cameron 2003, https://doi.org/10.1207/S15324834BASP2502_5). Each bubble is a counterargument forming. Once it forms, the reader is reading for the flaw.

    Answer the doubt in the next sentence

    • An off-ramp is a point where a doubt pulls the reader off your path.

    We ask whether a new solar farm lowers the sale prices of homes within one kilometer of it in upstate New York. Our sample covers the 431 farms that began operating between 2016 and 2023. Developers often build on cheap farmland at the edge of a village. Section 4 checks whether prices there were already growing more slowly. We compare prices near each farm with prices 2 to 5 kilometers away, before and after it opens.

    • One short sentence is enough. Name the doubt and point to where the paper handles it.
    • O’Keefe pooled 107 comparisons of messages that did or did not raise an opposing argument. Raising it and refuting it persuaded slightly more than ignoring it. Raising it without refuting it persuaded less.
    • Solution: answer each doubt briefly, right after the claim that raises it, and leave the full check for its own section.

    O’Keefe (1999), How to handle opposing arguments in persuasive messages: A meta-analytic review of the effects of one-sided and two-sided messages, Annals of the International Communication Association 22(1), 209–249.

    Two minutes.

    The green sentence closes the off-ramp that bubble 1 opened. It is short on purpose. It names the doubt and says where the paper deals with it, so the reader stays on the path, and it spends no time on how the check works. The full answer lives in section 4: the event study from meeting 9, which shows whether near and far prices drifted apart before each farm opened.

    This is how robustness checks usually enter a paper. One sentence where the doubt arises, “the estimate is similar when we drop farms on former farmland (appendix table A3)”, and the table in the appendix. A long answer in the middle of the argument is its own off-ramp.

    O’Keefe’s effects are small, and they hold outside advertising. Raising an objection and leaving it standing is worse than not raising it. Bubble 2 is answered by saying which farms the design covers; bubble 3 belongs in the limitations section, stated once.

    On-ramps bring drifting readers back

    • An on-ramp is a “you are here” sign: one sentence that tells a reader who drifted where this section fits in the whole argument.
    Without an on-ramp Sale records come from ATTOM through Dewey, Cornell’s data platform. We keep arm’s-length sales of single-family homes and drop sales below $10,000. Each address is geocoded and matched to the nearest farm.
    With an on-ramp To learn whether a new solar farm lowers the prices of nearby homes, we need the sale prices of homes near and far from each farm, before and after it opens. County averages would mix the two groups, so we need each sale with its address and date. We obtain these records from ATTOM through Dewey, Cornell’s data platform.
    • After their minds wandered, readers went back and reread nearly half the time, usually a line or two.
    • Talks need on-ramps too, most of all long ones: at each new part, say where it fits.
    • Solution: open each section with one sentence that tells the reader how it fits in the overall argument.

    Varao-Sousa, Solman and Kingstone (2017), Re-reading after mind wandering, Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology 71(3), 203–211.

    Two minutes.

    The data section is where most readers drift: sources, filters, merges. The first version is accurate and dry. It says where the data come from and what the team does to them, and a reader who drifted during the design section has no way back in.

    The green sentences are the on-ramp, a “you are here” sign on the map of the paper. They remind the reader of the question, say what kind of data it needs, and justify that choice: individual sales with addresses and dates, because county averages would mix homes near and far. Only then comes how the team gets them. A deep reader loses a few seconds; a reader who drifted rejoins with the argument in hand.

    Readers try to rejoin near where they left, so the rejoining point should be close: the start of each section, the first sentence of each paragraph. In talks the same tool is spoken. Titsworth and Kiewra (2004, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2003.12.001) had college students hear a lecture in which the speaker named the topic and category of each point; those students scored 15–45% higher on the tests. How to open a talk is meeting 12.

    Four ways off the path and their solutions

    Off the path Solution
    1 A criticism of the design Answer it briefly in the next sentence, and point to the full check
    2 A variation the reader wonders about Say which cases the design covers
    3 A limitation on where the result applies State it yourself, once, in the limitations section
    4 A reader who drifted Open each section with a “you are here” sentence: how it fits in the overall argument

    One minute.

    Rows 1 to 3 are the bubbles; row 4 is the on-ramp. One study behind row 3: in news stories about cancer research, 601 readers trusted the scientists more when the story reported limitations, and most when the scientists stated them themselves (Jensen 2008, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2008.00324.x). A limitation stated once, in its place, costs less than a hedge spread over every sentence.

    Spelling errors are an off-ramp of a cruder kind; the writing guide’s checklist covers proofreading.

    6. Plain and neutral sentences

    Thirty seconds. Sentences a reader can check: active verbs, numbers in place of adjectives, and one term for one thing.

    Hype words spread in funded grant abstracts

    • A hype word is an adjective or adverb that praises the work.
    With hype words We provide novel evidence on a critical question: the key role of solar farms in dramatically shaping local home values.
    With the comparison We estimate how much a new solar farm changes the sale prices of homes within one kilometer, in New York, where earlier studies have not looked.
    • Millar, Batalo and Budgell counted hype words in NIH-funded grant abstracts. The share with at least one rose from 72% in 1985 to 97% in 2020.
    • Some journals forbid claims to be first. Since 2017 Nature Human Behaviour has made authors remove them before acceptance.
    • Solution: replace each hype word with the comparison that shows it, and let the gap show that the work is new.

    Millar, Batalo and Budgell (2022), Trends in the use of promotional language (hype) in abstracts of successful National Institutes of Health grant applications, 1985–2020, JAMA Network Open 5(8), e2228676. Nature Human Behaviour (2021), Not the first, not the best, editorial, 5, 175.

    Two minutes.

    The first sentence in the box uses the three most common hype words Millar and coauthors found, plus an adverb. None of them can be checked. The second makes the same claim with the comparison: what is measured, where, and how it differs from earlier work. A reviewer can check every part of it.

    All the hype words they counted were adjectives. When nearly every funded abstract calls its work novel and critical, the words stop separating one application from another, and a careful reviewer starts discounting them.

    Some journals go further and remove the claim altogether. Nature Human Behaviour has required since 2017 that authors drop priority and novelty claims before acceptance, and since 2019 that they drop self-praising descriptions of their work; its reasons are that such claims are hard to verify and distract from the evidence. You do not need to write “this is the first study to”. If the introduction shows what is known and what is missing, the reader sees that you are filling the gap.

    Hype words went with higher odds of funding

    • Qiu and coauthors studied 11,535 grant applications: 2,439 to NIH from Northwestern faculty (2007–2019) and 9,096 to the Novo Nordisk Foundation, a Danish medical research funder (2015–2022).
    • They call them promotional words. Each extra percentage point went with about 1.5 times the odds of funding.
    • The data are observational. Work that is new may draw both the words and the funding.
    • A reviewer cannot check novel without a comparison.
    • Solution: say once what is new, compared with the closest published study.

    Qiu, Peng, Fosse, Woodruff and Uzzi (2024), Use of promotional language in grant applications and grant success, JAMA Network Open 7(12), e2448696.

    One and a half minutes.

    This is the evidence on the other side, and you should know it. The typical application used about 1% promotional words, so one extra percentage point roughly doubles the share. The authors cannot tell whether the words persuade or whether newer work is described with more of them.

    The fix keeps what the words were trying to do. Say once what is new, and show it with the comparison: the closest study, and what yours adds. Novelty is meeting 3’s test, and the closest study is meeting 5’s anchor paper.

    Give the number in place of the adjective

    Invented for this slide Target’s sales collapsed dramatically after the boycott.
    The “Target boycott” project description, verbatim “comparable store sales fell 4.3 percent in the second quarter of 2023”

    • The first sentence is made up to show the problem. The second is how the project description reports the figure, taken from Target’s earnings release.

    • Comparable store sales are in-store sales at stores open at least 13 months, against the same quarter a year earlier.

    • The number, its unit, the comparison and the period let the reader judge the size.

    Target Corporation (2023), second-quarter earnings release, Form 8-K, Exhibit 99, August 16. “Target boycott” project description.

    One and a half minutes.

    “Collapsed dramatically” asks the reader to trust the writer’s sense of scale. The second line lets the reader judge: 4.3 percent, against the same quarter a year earlier, in stores that were open both years. It also avoids a claim the first line makes without evidence, that the boycott caused the fall. Target said in 2023 that it could not separate the reaction from other causes.

    Three rules for verbs

    • Every sentence below is invented to show the rule. None corrects a published sentence.
    Rule Before (invented) After (invented)
    1 Active voice: the subject does the action, with we for the team Sale records were obtained from ATTOM through Dewey, Cornell’s data platform. We obtained sale records from ATTOM through Dewey, Cornell’s data platform.
    2 Present tense for what a paper or table shows, past for what happened in its sample, future for your plans Gaur and Lang (2023) found that solar farms lower home values. Gaur and Lang (2023) find that homes in Massachusetts and Rhode Island within 0.6 miles of a solar farm lost 1.5 to 3.6 percent of their value.
    3 Verbs in place of nominalizations, nouns made from verbs We conduct an analysis of sale prices. We analyze sale prices.

    The numbers in row 2 are Gaur and Lang’s finding. Gaur and Lang (2023), House of the rising sun: The effect of utility-scale solar arrays on housing prices, Energy Economics 122, 106699.

    One and a half minutes.

    The passive hides who did it. In a proposal the actor is the team, and “we” is standard in economics.

    The tense rule follows economics usage. A paper and a table show something now, so they take the present. What happened in a sample happened then, so it takes the past. Meeting 5 listed past to present as one way a draft claims more than the result. The “before” sentence does exactly that: it states a finding about homes in two states as a fact about solar farms everywhere. The present tense goes on the paper’s verb, never on the finding about its sample.

    Nominalizations turn the action into a noun and add a weak verb to carry it.

    Three rules for terms and claims

    • Every sentence below is invented to show the rule. None corrects a published sentence.
    Rule Before (invented) After (invented)
    1 One term for one thing Solar farm, array, facility and project in one paragraph read as four things. Solar farm, every time.
    2 Name the study and its setting The literature finds that solar farms lower home values. Elmallah et al. (2023) find that, across more than 1,500 solar projects in six states, homes within half a mile sold for about 1.5 percent less than homes two to four miles away.
    3 Hedge once, on the interpretation, where the doubt lies Visits may have fallen somewhat more in Republican counties, which possibly suggests the boycott may have mattered. Visits fell 2.1 percent more in the most Republican third of counties. The gap may reflect the boycott, or a local shock in the same weeks.

    The numbers in row 2 are Elmallah et al.’s finding; the 2.1 percent in row 3 is made up. McCloskey (1985), Economical Writing, Economic Inquiry 23(2), 187–222, rule 19. Elmallah, Hoen, Fujita, Robson and Brunner (2023), Shedding light on large-scale solar impacts, Energy Policy 175, 113425.

    One and a half minutes.

    A reader who meets a new word assumes a new thing. In economics prose, synonyms for variety cost the reader the most. McCloskey, an economist writing a style guide for economists, makes it rule 19.

    “The literature” hides how many studies, where and how large. Name the study, its setting and its number, and the reader can judge how far it carries to your setting.

    The hedge goes on the interpretation, once. The data statement stays plain: the team measured the gap, and the doubt is about what caused it.

    Cite what a reader could dispute

    • Cite any claim a reader could dispute. Mark a hypothesis or an opinion as yours, and point your own results to a table.

    1 Target’s comparable store sales fell 4.3 percent in the second quarter of 2023 (Target 2023). A number someone could check The source
    2 Homes within half a mile of a solar farm sold for about 1.5 percent less than homes two to four miles away (Elmallah et al. 2023). Another study’s finding The study
    3 Solar panels turn sunlight into electricity. Common knowledge Nothing

    Two minutes.

    The test for a citation is whether a reasonable reader could ask “says who?”. A number, a finding from another study, a method you take from someone else: each needs its source, at the sentence where it appears. Common knowledge does not: nobody needs a reference for what solar panels do.

    Your own claims come in two kinds, and the reader must be able to tell them apart. A hypothesis or an opinion is marked as yours, “we expect”, “we argue”, so it is not read as an established finding; if it rests on evidence, cite that evidence. Your own results point to the table or figure that shows them.

    The common failure runs both ways. A disputable claim with no source reads as the writer’s guess. A guess written in the voice of a finding, with no “we expect”, reads as a fact the writer has not earned.

    1. SectionsThe headings the document needs, in order
    2. OutlineUnder each heading, one bullet per paragraph: its topic sentence, and the tables, figures and sources it will cite
    3. ProseEach bullet becomes a paragraph

    • Every paragraph of the draft has its bullet in the outline.
    • Your proposal outline, due November 11, is pass 2.

    Meeting 5: “Plan first and work in small steps” and “You write the outline”.

    Two minutes.

    Pass 1 is short. For the proposal the headings are given: the course’s headings from section 3. For a paper, the schematic of an empirical paper is the default.

    Pass 2 does most of the thinking. Each bullet is one paragraph, and it holds the topic sentence written out in full, plus the evidence the paragraph will point to: the citation, the number, the table or figure. The outline is exhaustive. If a paragraph will exist, it has a bullet; if you cannot write the bullet, the paragraph has no point yet.

    This is how meeting 5 asked you to plan code: decide the structure first, then fill in small pieces you can check one at a time.

    Pass 3 is the writing, and it comes last. The proposal outline due on November 11 is exactly pass 2, which is why it is graded on its own.

      Seven steps for the proposal

      1. Name your readers, from the skimmer to the deep reader.
