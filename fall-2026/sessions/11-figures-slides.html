Visualizations have two jobs

  • An exploratory visualization is made by the author, for the author.
  • Exploratory ones are many, quick and rough. They find patterns and errors.
  • An explanatory visualization is made for a reader or a room.
  • Explanatory ones are few and finished. Each makes one point.
  • Meeting 5 covered exploring a new file. Today is about explaining.

Gelman and Unwin (2013), “Infovis and statistical graphics: different goals, different looks”, Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics 22(1).

Two minutes.

Meeting 5’s slide “Explore the data before you analyze it” covered the first job: what one row is, the units, how a missing value is written, and plotting the file before any model. Those figures are for you. You make dozens, you never polish them, and most are thrown away.

Gelman and Unwin argue the two jobs differ in their goals and should differ in their look. A figure made to discover something shows everything at once. A figure made to tell someone something makes one point and drops the rest. Knaflic’s Storytelling with Data draws the same line for business readers.

Everything after the outline is about the second kind.

Outline

A chart for a reader makes one point by showing numbers side by side. The point stands out by contrast with something else. Building the chart takes four steps.

  1. Decide what to contrast. The message, the benchmark, the quantity and the form.
  2. Encode it for the eye. Put the contrast where the eye reads it precisely.
  3. Keep it faithful. The scale, the window, the uncertainty and the title match the numbers.
  4. Make it stand alone. The title, the marks, the caption and the medium.
  5. Diagrams and builds. Draw how an idea works, and reveal it one piece at a time.
  6. Good charts and your proposal. Two that work, and what to draw before results.

Handout: the figure guide, on this meeting’s page.

Two minutes.

A number alone says little. A 12% rise in sales means more next to the regions without the program, the year before, or the target. To highlight something, a chart needs contrast with something else. Step 1 decides what that something else is, and steps 2 and 4 make the contrast visible. The finding can also be that there is no contrast, such as two lines that move together. That still takes the second line.

The four steps are also the order you work in. Each section opens with its four questions. The fifth section moves from data to ideas: a talk often has to explain a mechanism or a design before it can show a result. The figure guide on this meeting’s page has the same questions on one page. Session B uses it to critique figures, and your proposal figures should pass it.

1. Decide what to contrast

Twelve minutes for the section.

Four questions about what to contrast

  1. What is the message, in one sentence?
  2. Contrasted with what?
  3. Which quantity shows it?
  4. Which form shows it?

Zelazny (2001), Say It With Charts, 4th ed., McGraw-Hill: decide the message, find the comparison, then choose the chart.

One minute.

Zelazny was McKinsey’s director of visual communications, and the rule is the order of these four questions: the message decides what to contrast, and that decides the chart. A chart chosen first leaves the reader to find the message.

The choice of quantity decides what the reader contrasts. Nominal dollars compare the number printed on the paycheck. Real dollars compare what it buys.

One and a half minutes. Constant dollars was the first choice of quantity. These are three more, and each starts from the same data.

Levels show size, so the large chain always looks bigger. An index starts both chains at 100, so the reader sees which one grew faster.

Revenue and costs drawn as two lines make the reader subtract by eye. Profit draws the difference directly.

2. Encode it for the eye

Thirteen minutes for the section.

Readers can misread a chart in two ways

  • Encoding translates numbers into positions, lengths, angles, areas or colors.
  • Each of those is a channel: a visual property that stands for a number. A longer bar is a larger value.
  • A reader has to translate the marks back into numbers, and that can go wrong in two ways.

  • An imprecise reading scatters around the truth, as with a pie of close shares. Step 2 fixes it.
  • A biased reading misses in one direction, as with a bar axis that skips zero. Step 3 fixes it.

One and a half minutes. Start from the person looking at your chart. You translated numbers into marks, and that person has to translate the marks back into numbers. Everything in the next two sections is about whether that works.

The picture is an illustration with invented readers. Imagine 60 people each reading the same value, 50, off a chart. Each dot is one person’s answer.

On the left the answers are noisy. They center on the truth, but small differences get lost. On the right the answers agree with each other and all miss in the same direction, so the chart tells people something false.

These are the same two words as for estimators in meetings 6 and 7. This is also the rule that separates the next two sections. Choosing a pie over dots makes readings imprecise. Cutting a bar axis makes them biased.

Four questions about encoding

  1. Are the compared values on a common scale?
  2. Does the channel match the data?
  3. Are the compared items next to each other?
  4. Do the points hide each other?

Half a minute. Channel was defined on the last slide: the visual property that stands for the number. Question 2 asks whether the channel suits the kind of data: an ordered scale for ordered values, a line only along an ordered axis.

Question 4 has no slide today, so say it in a sentence. When thousands of points overlap, the plot hides how many lie where. A binned scatter plots the mean of y within equal-count bins of x. The figure guide has it, with the reference.

Readers judge position most accurately

Question 1: Are the compared values on a common scale?

  • Cleveland and McGill asked people what percent one marked value was of another.
  • Position on a common scale beat length, and length beat angle.
  • Heer and Bostock replicated it with 50 online workers per chart in 2010.
  • Circle and rectangle areas were read less accurately than angles.

Cleveland and McGill (1984), “Graphical perception”, Journal of the American Statistical Association 79. Heer and Bostock (2010), “Crowdsourcing graphical perception”, Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI).

Two minutes.

The figure draws the same two numbers, 40 and 60, five ways. Ask which one makes 60 easiest to read as one and a half times 40.

Cleveland and McGill ran two experiments at Bell Labs: one on bar and divided-bar charts, one on pie charts against bar charts. Subjects said which marked value was smaller and what percent it was of the larger. They tested position, length and angle. The rest of their ranking, from area down to color saturation, they drew from earlier perception research.

Heer and Bostock repeated the experiments on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk with 50 workers per chart, and added circles and rectangles. The ranking held, and both kinds of area were read least accurately.

Color is still the right channel for a map, where position is taken by geography. The next slides apply the ranking.

Ask the room which chain is second largest in the pie. Aldenmart (17%) and Corner Fresh (16%) are hard to separate by angle, and the legend sends the eye back and forth. The dot plot answers it at a glance, and it is sorted so the ranking is the order on the page.

Ordered values need an ordered color scale

Question 2: Does the channel match the data?

  • A map shows unemployment rates for every county in the lower 48 states.

As drawn

Redrawn

  • A rainbow scale has no order, and its hue changes draw false borders.
  • Borkin and coauthors tested artery images used to diagnose heart disease.
  • Viewers found 39% of problem areas in rainbow images and 91% in two-color ones.
  • Solution: use a scale that runs light to dark for ordered values.

Borkin et al. (2011), IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 17(12).

Two minutes.

In the rainbow map the eye sees bands where the hue changes from green to yellow or yellow to orange, though the rates change smoothly there. Nobody knows whether green is more than cyan. In the one-hue map, darker means higher, and the reader needs no legend to rank two counties.

Borkin and coauthors tested artery images used to diagnose heart disease. Participants looking for regions of low shear stress found 39% of them in 3D images with a rainbow scale and 91% in 2D images with a two-color diverging scale. Within 3D images the color scale alone made a large difference.

About 8% of men of European ancestry cannot tell red from green, which rules out the red and green ends of a rainbow too (Birch 2012, https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.29.000313).

Many series read best as small multiples

Question 3: Are the compared items next to each other?

  • The chart shows median home prices in ten Texas cities from 2000 to 2014.

As drawn

Redrawn

  • Many series in one panel make each line hard to follow.
  • Small multiples give each series its own small panel on shared axes.
  • Javed and coauthors found them faster for comparisons across a long period.
  • Solution: draw small multiples when each series has its own story.

Javed, McDonnel and Elmqvist (2010), IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 16(6).

Two minutes.

Each small panel shows one city in red against the others in grey, on the same axes, so the reader sees each city’s path and where it sits among the rest. Austin stays the most expensive; El Paso flattens after 2007.

Javed and coauthors had 16 students compare 2, 4 or 8 series drawn in one panel, as small multiples, and two other ways. Small multiples were faster when the comparison spanned much of the time axis. One shared panel was better for comparing values at a single date.

Schwabish (2014) makes the same redraw for a published chart of disability awards by cause, 1975 to 2010.

3. Keep it faithful

Seventeen minutes for the section.

Four questions about faithfulness

  1. Does the scale match the quantity?
  2. Do the window and the sample favor the message?
  3. Is the uncertainty shown?
  4. Does the title claim only what the figure shows?

Half a minute. A faithfulness problem biases the reading: readers come away believing something the numbers do not say.

Truncated axes make a change look more serious

Question 1: Does the scale match the quantity?

  • Correll, Bertini and Franconeri ran three online experiments.
  • Readers rated how serious a change looked, on a scale from 1 to 5.
  • Starting the axis at 25% in place of zero raised the rating by 0.36 points.
  • An axis break did not remove the effect.
  • The effect stayed when readers read the values correctly.
  • Pandey and coauthors found the same for truncated bar charts.

Correll, Bertini and Franconeri (2020), “Truncating the y-axis: threat or menace?”, Proceedings of CHI. Pandey et al. (2015), “How deceptive are deceptive visualizations?”, Proceedings of CHI.

One and a half minutes.

Participants were recruited online and saw many bar and line charts of the same change, with the y-axis starting at zero, at 25% or at 50%. Starting at 25% raised the rated severity by 0.36 points on the five-point scale, for bars and lines alike. Broken-axis symbols and gradient bars did not remove it. People who could read the exact values off the axis still judged the change larger.

Pandey and coauthors found the same with truncated bar charts in a separate experiment with crowd workers.

Bars start at zero

Question 1: Does the scale match the quantity?

  • In July 2012 Fox Business showed the top income tax rate if the 2001 tax cuts expired.

As drawn: axis from 34%

Redrawn from zero

  • A truncated bar drew a 4.6-point rise as a bar 5.6 times taller.
  • In 2015 the Obama White House drew graduation rates as books with no axis.
  • Solution: start bars at zero, or show the values as dots.

Redrawn from the published numbers. Media Matters, “Dishonest Fox chart”, 2012. Quartz, “The most misleading charts of 2015, fixed”.

Two and a half minutes.

The Fox Business chart aired on Cavuto on July 31, 2012. It showed the top income tax rate at 35% “now” and 39.6% on January 1, 2013, if the 2001 tax cuts expired, on an axis starting at 34%. The second bar’s visible part is 5.6 / 1 = 5.6 times the first.

The Obama White House tweeted a chart on December 16, 2015, showing the high-school graduation rate at an all-time high, drawn as columns of stacked books with no visible y-axis. Quartz redrew it from zero and the rise looked much smaller. The fault belongs to no party. Session B opens on this chart.

Bars need zero because readers compare their lengths. A line or a dot plot can start elsewhere, because readers compare positions. The test is whether the axis matches the quantity: an index, a temperature or a growth rate has no natural zero to show.

Readers misread COVID-19 deaths on a log scale

Question 1: Does the scale match the quantity?

  • In April 2020, 2,074 Americans saw this chart, half on each scale, at random.

Log scale

Linear scale

  • The log group read the trend less accurately, forecast worse, and wanted businesses reopened sooner.
  • Solution: use a linear scale for a general reader.

Romano, Sotis, Dominioni and Guidi (2020), “The scale of COVID-19 graphs affects understanding, attitudes, and policy preferences”, Health Economics 29(11). Redrawn from the Johns Hopkins University daily counts, CC BY 4.0.

Two minutes. Ask the room which chart looks more alarming before the click. Both draw the same Johns Hopkins counts, March 1 to April 30, 2020: about 67,000 deaths by the end of April.

A log scale shows equal percentage changes as equal distances, so it is the right scale for growth rates. Most readers do not know that. In this experiment, published in an economics journal, the participants were recruited online and shown the worldometers chart of US deaths. Those who saw the log version judged which week rose more less accurately, made higher forecasts that were further from the truth, were less worried, and favored reopening businesses sooner.

The scale changed policy preferences. If your reader is not a specialist, use a linear scale, or say on the figure that each gridline is ten times the last.

Two periods belong on one scale

Question 2: Do the window and the sample favor the message?

  • A chart shared in February 2014 overlaid the 1929 Dow on 2012–14 to warn of a crash.

The overlay’s method

One scale, the full period

  • This redraw uses the S&P composite stock index from 1927 and from 2012.
  • Two y-axes let the author set where the lines meet. A chosen window lines them up.
  • Solution: put compared periods on one scale, over the full period.

Redrawn with Shiller’s monthly S&P composite. Quartz (2014), on the original Dow chart. Shiller, Irrational Exuberance data.

Two and a half minutes.

The original chart circulated in February 2014 among market analysts (Tom McClellan, Tom DeMark) and ran on MarketWatch on February 11. It drew the Dow in 1928–29 on one axis and the Dow in 2012–14 on another, with the start dates and both axis ranges chosen so the two lines matched. The 1929 crash then sat just ahead of the 2014 line.

Our redraw uses Robert Shiller’s monthly S&P composite, not the daily Dow, with the same method: the two axes are fitted so the 26 shared months line up. On one scale, indexed to 100 in each first month, the 1927–29 market rose 134% before the crash; 2012–14 rose 40%. No crash followed in 2014 or 2015.

Two y-axes are not always wrong, but whoever chooses both ranges chooses where the lines cross. No experiment we found measures this; the mechanism is enough.

Estimates need their intervals

Question 3: Is the uncertainty shown?

  • The chain estimates the loyalty program’s effect in each of its 8 launch regions.

As drawn

Redrawn

  • Uncertainty left out: the reader cannot tell which effects differ from zero.
  • A 95% confidence interval (CI) covers the true effect in 95% of samples.
  • An error bar shows a standard error or a confidence interval.
  • Four of the eight intervals include zero.
  • Solution: draw each interval, and say in the caption which interval it is.

Two minutes.

Without intervals, the first figure says the program raised sales in seven of eight regions. With 95% confidence intervals, only four regions’ effects are distinguishable from zero. Meeting 6 defined the confidence interval and the standard error.

Say which interval you drew. A standard-error bar is about half the length of a 95% interval, and readers confuse the two. Belia and coauthors (https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.10.4.389) asked 473 published researchers to read error bars, and many mixed them up.

Authors know they leave this out. Hullman (https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2019.2934287) surveyed 90 authors of data visualizations, and 62% of those who omit uncertainty said they feared confusing readers.

A causal title needs a causal design

Question 4: Does the title claim only what the figure shows?

  • In 2012 a cardiologist plotted chocolate against Nobel laureates for 23 countries.

The title the news gave it

The title the figure supports

  • The correlation was 0.79, and several news outlets reported it as cause and effect.
  • A causal title on a correlation claims more than the figure shows.
  • Solution: title the association, and leave causal claims to a design.

Messerli (2012), “Chocolate consumption, cognitive function, and Nobel laureates”, New England Journal of Medicine 367(16). The paper’s figure is behind a paywall; the points here are invented in its pattern.

Two minutes. The points are invented, and the chart says so, because the paper’s figure cannot be reproduced here. The pattern is the paper’s: 23 countries, r about 0.8, a few chocolate-loving countries at the top.

Franz Messerli published this as an “Occasional Notes” piece, not a peer-reviewed study. Chocolate consumption came from industry data and the laureate counts from a per-capita ranking. The figure plots one dot per country; Switzerland and Sweden sit at the top. The correlation was 0.791 (0.862 without Sweden). The methods mention 22 countries with chocolate data; news reports said 23. He told Reuters the idea was absurd but the data legitimate, and outlets still reported it straight.

Richer countries eat more chocolate and fund more research. A title such as “Countries that eat more chocolate have more Nobel laureates” states what the figure shows. A title such as “Chocolate makes countries smarter” needs a design from meetings 8 and 9.

Readers remember the message in the title

Question 1: Does the title state the finding?

  • Kong, Liu and Karahalios showed 104 Americans this chart, each with one of its two titles.

Title for one side

Title for the other side

  • 77 of 101 recalled messages matched the title, and about 80% called the chart neutral either way.
  • Borkin and coauthors tracked eyes on 393 charts; viewers restated the title in 46% of descriptions.

Kong, Liu and Karahalios (2018), “Frames and slants in titles of visualizations on controversial topics”, Proceedings of CHI. Borkin et al. (2016), “Beyond memorability”, IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 22(1). Chart redrawn from the study’s Figure 1a, data from UNHCR, April 2017.

Two minutes.

Each participant saw two charts, one on Syrian refugee admissions and one on the US defense budget. Each chart had two series that supported opposite policy positions, and a title slanted toward one. The refugee chart is redrawn here from the numbers on the study’s own figure, which the authors adapted from a chart in The Atlantic. Both titles are the study’s, word for word. Read them aloud, one before the click and one after: the chart does not change. Asked later what the chart said, most repeated the title’s side. Most also judged the chart neutral, and their own attitudes did not change. A follow-up (2019) found the same when the title contradicted the chart.

Borkin and coauthors tracked the eyes of 33 people looking at 393 published visualizations for ten seconds each. Titles drew 59% of fixations and were restated in 46% of the descriptions people wrote afterwards.

The title is what most readers keep. That cuts both ways: it carries your finding, and it can carry a claim the data do not support (the chocolate slide).

The title states the finding

Question 1: Does the title state the finding?

Label title

Finding title

  • A label title names the variables and leaves the finding to the reader.
  • Academic papers mostly use label titles, like the left panel, and state the finding in the text.
  • For a general audience, in a talk, the finding goes in the figure title, or in the slide title.
  • Solution: write the finding as the title, and put the variables in the subtitle.

One and a half minutes.

Journals mostly print label titles, or a caption that starts with one, and the finding sits in the text. That convention is fine for a reader who will read the text. A room will not, and neither will a manager who looks only at the figure. For them, write the finding on the figure, and in a talk the slide title can carry it, as meeting 12 asks of every slide. Meeting 10 made the same point for paper titles: a title that states the result reaches the readers who read nothing else.

5. Diagrams and builds

Eleven minutes for the section.

A diagram shows how the parts of an idea connect

  • A chart compares values. A diagram shows how the parts of an idea connect.
  • Three forms recur in applied work.

Causal graph, meeting 2

Research design, meeting 9

Data pipeline

  • Larkin and Simon modeled people solving physics problems about pulleys.
  • A diagram keeps related facts in one place, so the solver searches less.

Larkin and Simon (1987), “Why a diagram is (sometimes) worth ten thousand words”, Cognitive Science 11(1).

One and a half minutes.

Everything so far compared values. A talk or a proposal also has to explain how something works, and that takes a different picture. You have seen two of these already: the causal graphs in meeting 2 and the staggered-adoption grid in meeting 9. The data pipeline is new. It tells a reader where the estimation sample came from.

Larkin and Simon’s paper is a computer model of problem solving, with no experiment on people. Two representations can hold the same facts and differ in the work they ask of the reader. In a list of sentences the reader hunts for the next fact. In a diagram the next fact is often next to the last one. Their warning applies today: an arbitrary diagram does not earn this advantage. It has to be drawn for the argument.

Four questions about diagrams

  1. Which parts does the claim need?
  2. Does each arrow style carry one meaning?
  3. Can you defend every arrow?
  4. Does it build in the order of the argument?

Half a minute.

Every arrow is a claim

  • A team draws its project on the loyalty program, from the pilot to the estimate.

As drawn

Redrawn

  • Identical arrows hide that these links mean four different things.
  • A causal arrow claims an effect, and a missing arrow claims there is none.
  • Solution: give each meaning its own style and label, and defend every causal arrow.

One and a half minutes.

Read the first diagram aloud as a chain: the pilot leads to the launch, which leads to visits, then sales, scanner data and the estimate. Read that way it says the pilot caused the launch and sales caused the scanner data. Only two of the five arrows are causal.

A whole diagram at once loses the room

  • The room reads ahead while the speaker explains the first arrow.
  • Mayer and Chandler taught students how lightning forms with a narrated animation.
  • Students who saw it in 16 short segments scored higher on new problems, a transfer test.
  • Solution: reveal one piece for each sentence of the argument, and cut any piece no sentence uses.

Mayer and Chandler (2001), “When learning is just a click away”, Journal of Educational Psychology 93(2). Rey et al. (2019), “A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect”, Educational Psychology Review 31(2).

One and a half minutes.

Leave the diagram up in silence for a few seconds, then ask where people started reading. Six parts and seven arrows arrive at once, and each person starts somewhere different. By the time you have explained one arrow, they are working on another.

Mayer and Chandler’s animation ran 140 seconds. The segmented version cut it into 16 pieces of eight to ten seconds, each with a Continue button. Students who saw the segments did better on transfer and no better on recall of the facts. Rey and coauthors pool later studies and find small to medium effects.

Those students clicked at their own pace, and a talk moves at the speaker’s pace. So this is evidence for explaining in pieces. I have not seen a clean experiment on slide builds in a talk, and the case for them also rests on experience.

A paper’s reader sets the pace, so a paper prints the whole diagram.

A causal chain builds one link at a time

  • Each step adds one link and one sentence. Red arrows are the effects, as in meeting 2.

Two minutes. One click for each sentence, and say nothing about a piece before it appears.

  1. The program gives members a reason to come back, so visits per customer rise.
  2. More visits mean more sales, and sales add to profit.
  3. The program also hands out discounts, and discounts come out of profit. So the effect on profit could go either way.
  4. Suppose the chain launched first where growth looked strongest. Regional growth then causes both the launch and sales. That is a back door (meeting 2), and it is why the next slide needs a design.

The last frame is the slide you just saw. The room can read it now.

To make one: in PowerPoint or Keynote, draw the finished diagram and give each piece an Appear animation. In Quarto, put the fragment class on each frame. In R, save one image per frame with the same limits.

A research design builds one piece at a time

  • Difference-in-differences compares the change in launch regions with the change in other regions.
  • The axes and positions stay fixed, so only the new piece moves the eye.

Two minutes. This is a schematic with no data, so the y-axis has no numbers. Meeting 9 taught the design. Here the lesson is the telling.

  1. Sales in the launch regions rose after the launch.
  2. Sales in the other regions rose too, so part of that rise has nothing to do with the program.
  3. If the launch regions had changed like the other regions, they would have ended here. This dashed line is parallel trends from meeting 9. It is an assumption, and the room now sees exactly where it enters.
  4. The gap between the solid line and the dashed line is the estimate.

If the axis limits change from frame to frame, the lines jump and the room loses its place. Fix the limits first, then add the layers.

Rosling (2006), “The best stats you’ve ever seen”, TED. Redrawn from World Bank data: each bubble is a country, with area proportional to population.

One and a half minutes. Easiest slide to drop in this section.

The same habit works on a chart with real data. Rosling’s students pictured a world of two groups: long lives and small families, short lives and large families. The talk first says what the axes are, what a bubble is and what its size means. Only then do the years run, and the room can follow every country because it already knows how to read the picture.

Our World in Data, “Life expectancy vs. health spending”, CC BY. U.S. Energy Information Administration, Today in Energy, November 17, 2025, public domain.

Two minutes. Run the steps on each chart, out loud, and let the room answer.

Our World in Data. The message is that the United States spends far more than any other country and lives shorter lives than the rich countries it resembles. The benchmark is every other country. The quantity is adjusted for inflation and for differences in living costs. Readers judge position on two common scales, and the country names sit on the points. The axes skip zero, which is fine for points. The note says which years stand in where data are missing. The one thing to change is the title: state the finding.

Energy Information Administration. The Permian Basin lies in west Texas and southeastern New Mexico. Rigs and barrels have different units. A second y-axis would let the author choose where the lines cross, and two panels on one time axis remove that choice. The reader still sees rigs falling while output rises. The article’s headline carries the finding, and the chart title is a label. The same article also has a weaker chart, which one team meets in a few minutes.

Four steps for every chart

1. Decide what to contrast

  1. What is the message?
  2. Contrasted with what?
  3. Which quantity?
  4. Which form?

2. Encode it for the eye

  1. Are the compared values on a common scale?
  2. Does the channel match the data?
  3. Are compared items together?
  4. Do the points hide each other?

3. Keep it faithful

  1. Does the scale match the quantity?
  2. Do the window and sample favor the message?
  3. Is the uncertainty shown?
  4. Is the title supported?

4. Make it stand alone

  1. Does the title state the finding?
  2. Does every mark carry information?
  3. Does the caption stand alone?
  4. Does it fit the page or slide?

Diagrams: cut unused parts, give each arrow style one meaning, defend every arrow, and build in the order of the argument.
